# From Cattle to Credit
# 從牛到信用

> **Put financial English in historical order and it tells the story of money itself: from cattle you could count, to a bench you could sit at, to a promise you have to believe.**
>
> **把金融英文按歷史排好，它就是一部錢的歷史：從數得出來的牛，到坐得上去的長凳，再到只能相信的承諾。**

## 1. Your Fee Used to Moo｜你付的費用，祖先是一頭牛

Every time you pay a fee, whether a bank fee, a school fee or a lawyer's fee, you are using a word whose ancestor was a cow. In Old English, *feoh* meant livestock. Over time it widened to mean property in general, then wealth, and finally money. Its relatives show the same range: German *Vieh* still means livestock, while Gothic *faihu* meant money and fortune.

每次你付一筆 fee，不管是銀行手續費、學費還是律師費，你用的這個字，祖先都是一頭牛。古英語的 *feoh* 指的是牲畜，後來擴大成一般財產，再變成財富，最後變成錢。它的親戚也橫跨同樣的範圍：德語的 *Vieh* 到今天還是「牲畜」，哥德語的 *faihu* 則是「錢、財富」。

The word did not travel alone. Linguists trace *feoh* to an older root, \*peku-, "livestock," which also gave Latin *pecu* (cattle) and *pecunia* (money). That is why English has *pecuniary*, "relating to money," and *impecunious*, "having no money." At the root, an impecunious person is someone without cattle.

這個字不是單獨上路的。語言學家把 *feoh* 追溯到更古老的詞根 \*peku-（牲畜），它也產生了拉丁語的 *pecu*（牛）和 *pecunia*（錢）。所以英文有 *pecuniary*（金錢上的）和 *impecunious*（身無分文的）。追到根部，impecunious 的人就是「沒有牛的人」。

The *fee* you pay today also took a detour through the feudal world. Around 1300, English borrowed the Anglo-French sense of a fee as land held from a lord. From there it came to mean the income attached to an office, and eventually the charge for a service.

今天你付的 *fee*，中間還繞道經過封建世界。大約 1300 年，英語從盎格魯法語借來 fee 的另一個意思：向領主領受的封地。之後它變成「某個職位附帶的收入」，最後才變成「服務的費用」。

## 2. Counting Heads｜數「頭」

Latin *caput* means "head." From it came the adjective *capitalis*, "chief, principal," and in Medieval Latin the noun *capitale*, "property" or "stock": the principal thing a person owned. English still counts livestock by the head, as in "fifty head of cattle," which makes this family easy to remember.

拉丁語的 *caput* 是「頭」。從它衍生出形容詞 *capitalis*（主要的、首要的），到了中世紀拉丁語又有名詞 *capitale*（財產、資產），也就是一個人最主要的家當。英文到現在還用「頭」來數牲畜，例如 fifty head of cattle（五十頭牛），這讓這一家人特別好記。

From *capitale*, English received the same word three times. *Capital*, first recorded in the 1610s as "a person's wealth," later came to mean money put to work in a business. *Cattle* arrived in the 13th century meaning property of any kind, including money and land; it narrowed to "livestock" in the 1300s and only from the late 1500s meant cows and bulls. *Chattel*, the legal word for movable property, is cattle's twin from the same source. When an economist talks about capital and a farmer talks about cattle, they are using the same word.

英語從 *capitale* 收到了三次同一個字。*Capital* 在 1610 年代首次出現，意思是「個人的財富」，後來才變成「投入事業的資金」。*Cattle* 在 13 世紀進入英語，原本指任何財產，連錢和土地都算；14 世紀縮小成「牲畜」，直到 16 世紀後期才專指牛。*Chattel*（動產）是法律用語，和 cattle 是同源的雙胞胎。所以經濟學家說 capital、農夫說 cattle，其實用的是同一個字。

Chinese has a neat parallel. The 本 in 資本 and 本錢 originally meant the root of a tree; the Shuowen dictionary defines it as "the part below the tree." Latin chose the head, Chinese chose the root, and both picked the part that everything else depends on. This is a parallel in thinking, not a shared origin.

中文有個巧妙的對照。「資本」「本錢」的「本」，本義是樹根，《說文解字》說「木下曰本」。拉丁語選了頭，中文選了根，兩者都挑了「其他一切都依靠它」的那個部位。這是思維上的平行，不是同一個來源。

## 3. Money from a Temple｜從神廟來的錢

The word *money* comes from Latin *moneta*, which meant both "a mint" and "coined money." *Moneta* was also a title of the goddess Juno, and Rome's coins were struck near her temple on the Capitoline Hill. The name of the place became the name of what was made there, and later the name of wealth in general.

*Money* 來自拉丁語 *moneta*，意思同時是「鑄幣廠」和「鑄好的錢」。*Moneta* 也是女神 Juno 的稱號，羅馬的錢幣就在她位於卡比托利歐山的神廟附近鑄造。地方的名字變成了在那裡生產的東西的名字，後來又變成一切財富的名字。

Why was Juno called *Moneta*? The traditional answer links it to *monere*, "to warn," making her "the goddess who warns." It is an appealing idea, but etymologists consider the origin uncertain, so it is best told as tradition, not fact.

Juno 為什麼叫 *Moneta*？傳統說法把它連到 *monere*（警告），所以她是「提出警告的女神」。這個說法很吸引人，但詞源學家認為來源並不確定，所以只能當作傳統說法，不能當成定論。

Chinese money took a different path: it came from the sea. The character 貝 is a picture of a cowrie shell, and shells served as currency in early China. That is why so many money characters contain 貝: 財 (wealth), 貨 (goods), 買 and 賣 (buy and sell), 貴 (expensive), 貸 (lend) and 資 (capital). The Shuowen, compiled around AD 100, says it plainly: in ancient times shells were used as money, and the Qin abolished shells in favour of coins. English money was counted in cattle; Chinese money was counted in shells.

中文的錢走了另一條路：它來自海裡。「貝」是海貝的象形字，古代中國以貝殼為貨幣。所以許多和錢有關的字都帶著「貝」：財、貨、買、賣、貴、貸、資。約成書於西元 100 年的《說文解字》說得很直接：「古者貨貝而寶龜，周而有泉，至秦廢貝行錢。」英文的錢用牛來數，中文的錢用貝殼來數。

## 4. A Bench, and a Broken Bench｜一張長凳，一張破長凳

A *bank* began as furniture. Old Italian *banca* meant a table or bench, specifically the counter where money-dealers did business. The financial sense reached English in the late 1400s, and the meaning "an institution that receives and lends money" followed in the 1620s. *Bench* and the *bank* of a river are relatives too, but all three descend in parallel from one Germanic root, so neither *bank* comes from the other.

*Bank* 最早是一件家具。古義大利語的 *banca* 是桌子或長凳，特別指錢商做生意的檯面。這個金融用法在 15 世紀後期進入英語，「收受與放貸金錢的機構」這個意思則到 1620 年代才出現。*Bench*（長凳）和河岸的 *bank* 也是親戚，但三者是從同一個日耳曼詞根平行分出來的，所以銀行的 bank 和河岸的 bank 並不是誰衍生出誰。

*Bankrupt* comes from Italian *banca rotta*, "broken bench," and entered English in the 1560s. A popular story says that when a money-dealer failed, his bench was publicly smashed. Etymologists doubt it: the "broken bench" was most likely a figure of speech for a business that had collapsed. The word is real; the ceremony probably is not.

*Bankrupt* 來自義大利語 *banca rotta*（破掉的長凳），1560 年代進入英語。有個流傳很廣的故事說，錢商倒閉時，他的長凳會被當眾砸爛。詞源學家對此存疑：「破長凳」很可能只是比喻生意垮了。這個字是真的，那個儀式大概不是。

## 5. Put Your Heart on It｜把心放上去

*Credit* entered English in the 1540s meaning "belief" or "trust." It comes, through French and Italian, from Latin *creditum*, "a thing entrusted to another," from *credere*, "to believe, to trust." Go one step further back and *credere* means, literally, "to put one's heart" on something. The same root gives *creed* and *credo*, statements of what you believe.

*Credit* 在 1540 年代進入英語，意思是「信念、信任」。它經由法語和義大利語，來自拉丁語 *creditum*（託付給別人的東西），源頭是 *credere*（相信、託付）。再往前追一步，*credere* 的字面意思是「把心放上去」。同一個詞根還產生了 *creed* 和 *credo*，也就是「信條」。

So every credit card, credit rating and credit market rests on a word for belief. When people say that credit "dried up" in a crisis, the etymology is exact: lenders stopped believing.

所以每一張信用卡、每一個信用評等、每一個信用市場，底下都是一個代表「相信」的字。危機時大家說信用「枯竭」，從詞源看再精準不過：放款人不再相信了。

Chinese again offers a parallel rather than a relative. The 信 in 信用 is made of 人 (person) and 言 (words), and the Shuowen glosses it as "sincerity": a person standing by what they say. Latin put its heart on the line; Chinese put its word on the line.

中文這裡又提供了一個平行對照，而不是親戚。「信用」的「信」由「人」和「言」組成，《說文》說「信，誠也」：一個人說話算話。拉丁語押上的是心，中文押上的是話。

*Debt* tells a smaller, stranger story. In Middle English it was spelled *dette*, borrowed from French, with no *b* at all. Later, scholars who admired Latin added a *b* to the spelling to show that the word came from *debitum*, "something owed." Nobody ever pronounced it. The silent *b* in *debt* is, fittingly, something the spelling still owes to Latin.

*Debt* 的故事比較小，也比較怪。中古英語從法語借來時拼成 *dette*，根本沒有 *b*。後來崇拜拉丁文的學者在拼字裡加了一個 *b*，好表明這個字來自 *debitum*（欠下的東西）。從來沒有人念出這個 b。說起來很貼切：*debt* 裡那個不發音的 *b*，正是英文拼字至今還欠著拉丁文的一筆債。

## 6. The Dead Pledge｜死亡抵押

*Mortgage* comes from Old French *mort gaige*, literally "dead pledge," and entered English in the late 14th century. Why dead? The 17th-century English jurist Sir Edward Coke explained that the pledge dies either way. If the borrower fails to pay on the agreed day, the land is lost to him for good, so the pledge is dead to him. If he pays, the pledge is finished, so it is dead to the lender.

*Mortgage* 來自古法語 *mort gaige*，字面意思是「死亡的抵押」，14 世紀後期進入英語。為什麼是「死」的？17 世紀的英國法學家 Sir Edward Coke 解釋：這個抵押無論如何都會死。借款人到期沒還，土地就永遠歸對方，抵押對他來說死了；借款人還清了，抵押也就結束了，對放款人來說死了。

It is a grim name, but a precise one: a mortgage is a promise designed to end, one way or the other.

這個名字很陰森，卻很精準：房貸就是一個注定要結束的承諾，只是看用哪一種方式結束。

## 7. Finance Means "The End"｜finance 的意思是「結束」

*Finance* arrived around 1400 meaning "an ending" or "a settlement." It comes from Old French *finer*, "to end, to settle a debt," from *fin*, "end," and ultimately Latin *finis*. The modern sense, "the management of money," appeared only in 1770. The *fine* you pay for a parking ticket comes from the same root: a payment that brings a matter to an end. Finance, at heart, is the business of settling things.

*Finance* 大約在 1400 年進入英語，意思是「結束」或「了結」。它來自古法語 *finer*（結束、清償債務），源頭是 *fin*（終點），再往前是拉丁語 *finis*。「管理金錢」這個現代意思，直到 1770 年才出現。你繳的停車罰單 *fine* 也來自同一個詞根：付一筆錢，讓事情了結。說到底，金融就是「了結事情」的行業。

## 8. The Salt Myth｜鹽的迷思

You have probably heard that *salary* comes from salt because Roman soldiers were paid in it. Only half of that holds up. *Salary* does come from Latin *salarium*, an allowance or stipend, from the adjective *salarius*, "of salt." But no ancient source says soldiers were paid in salt, and even the gentler version, a "salt allowance" for buying salt, is a modern guess. The link between the word and salt is real; the story built on that link has no evidence behind it.

你大概聽過：*salary* 來自鹽，因為羅馬士兵領鹽當薪水。這句話只有一半站得住。*Salary* 確實來自拉丁語 *salarium*（津貼、薪俸），源自形容詞 *salarius*（與鹽有關的）。但沒有任何古代文獻說士兵領鹽當薪水，就連比較溫和的版本「給士兵買鹽的津貼」，也只是近代的推測。字和鹽的關係是真的，建立在這個關係上的故事卻沒有證據。

It is a useful lesson for any word learner: a true etymology can still carry a false story. Check the story separately from the word.

這對所有學單字的人都是一課：詞源是真的，附在上面的故事仍然可能是假的。字要查，故事也要分開查。

## 9. Reading the Whole Story｜讀出整個故事

Put these words in order and you get a short history of money. First it walked: *fee*, *pecuniary*, *capital*, *cattle*. Then it was struck in a temple: *money*. Then it sat on a bench: *bank*, *bankrupt*. Finally it became a promise: *credit*, *debt*, *mortgage*, settled in the end by *finance*. Much of modern finance runs on that last kind of money, a promise we agree to believe. For an investor, that is the step to watch: when belief goes, credit goes with it.

把這些字按順序排好，就是一部簡短的錢的歷史。一開始，錢會走路：*fee*、*pecuniary*、*capital*、*cattle*。接著，錢在神廟裡被鑄造：*money*。然後，錢坐上了長凳：*bank*、*bankrupt*。最後，錢變成了承諾：*credit*、*debt*、*mortgage*，由 *finance* 來了結。現代金融有很大一部分靠的就是最後這一種錢：一個大家同意相信的承諾。對投資人來說，這正是要盯緊的地方：信任一消失，信用就跟著消失。

## Quick Check｜小測驗

1. What did *fee* originally mean? ｜ *fee* 最早是什麼意思？
   (a) a tax 稅 (b) livestock 牲畜 (c) a coin 硬幣
2. Which story do etymologists doubt? ｜ 哪個故事被詞源學家存疑？
   (a) *bank* came from a word for bench (b) failed money-dealers had their benches smashed (c) *credit* comes from a word for believe
3. Why is there a *b* in *debt*? ｜ *debt* 裡為什麼有 *b*？
   (a) It used to be pronounced. (b) Scholars added it to match Latin *debitum*. (c) It is a printing error.
4. Which statement about *salary* is accurate? ｜ 關於 *salary*，哪一句正確？
   (a) It is linked to salt, and soldiers were paid in salt. (b) It is linked to salt, but there is no evidence soldiers were paid in salt. (c) It has nothing to do with salt.

### Answers｜解答

1. **(b)** Old English *feoh* meant livestock before it meant money. ｜ 古英語 *feoh* 先指牲畜，後來才指錢。
2. **(b)** The "broken bench" was probably a figure of speech. ｜「破長凳」很可能只是比喻。
3. **(b)** The *b* was never pronounced; it was added to point back to Latin. ｜ 這個 *b* 從來不發音，是為了指向拉丁文而加的。
4. **(b)** The word is linked to *sal*, but the soldier story has no ancient evidence. ｜ 字和 *sal*（鹽）有關，但士兵領鹽的故事沒有古代證據。
